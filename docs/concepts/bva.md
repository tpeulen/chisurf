# Burst Variance Analysis (BVA)

Burst Variance Analysis (BVA) is a **model-free test for sub-burst FRET
dynamics** in single-molecule confocal experiments. It asks a simple question of
each detected burst: *is the FRET efficiency constant while the molecule crosses
the confocal volume, or does it change?* A genuinely static molecule can only
vary its apparent efficiency because of **shot noise** — the finite, random way a
fixed number of photons partitions into donor and acceptor counts. A molecule
that interconverts between conformations *during* the burst adds **excess**
variance on top of that shot-noise floor. BVA makes that excess visible.

For the step-by-step workflow in ChiSurf, see the guide
{doc}`/guides/08_burst_variance_analysis`.

## The idea: split each burst and measure the scatter

A burst is a stream of photons collected while one molecule is in the focus.
Instead of collapsing the whole burst to a single efficiency, BVA **slices** the
burst into short consecutive sub-windows of $n$ photons each. For every slice it
computes the proximity ratio (apparent FRET efficiency)

$$
E_i = \frac{n_A^{(i)}}{n_A^{(i)} + n_D^{(i)}} = \frac{n_A^{(i)}}{n},
$$

where $n_A^{(i)}$ and $n_D^{(i)}$ are the acceptor and donor photon counts in
slice $i$. The BVA statistic for that burst is the **standard deviation of the
per-slice efficiencies**,

$$
s_E = \sqrt{\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\bigl(E_i - \bar{E}\bigr)^2},
$$

taken over the $m$ slices of the burst. Each burst therefore contributes a single
point $(\bar{E},\, s_E)$ to a two-dimensional plot.

## The shot-noise floor: a static molecule's expected scatter

If the molecule is static during the burst, every slice draws its $n_A$ acceptor
photons from the *same* underlying efficiency $E$. The acceptor count is then a
**binomial** random variable, $n_A \sim \mathrm{Binomial}(n, E)$, so the per-slice
efficiency $E_i = n_A/n$ has variance $E(1-E)/n$. The expected standard deviation
is the shot-noise limit

$$
\boxed{\;\sigma_\text{sn}(E) = \sqrt{\dfrac{E\,(1-E)}{n}}\;}
$$

Plotted against $E$ this is a **semicircle-like curve** — zero at $E=0$ and $E=1$
(where the outcome is nearly deterministic) and largest at $E=\tfrac12$ — that
narrows as the slice size $n$ grows. It is the **static line** (also "BVA line"):
the scatter you *must* observe from shot noise alone, with no dynamics
whatsoever. In ChiSurf the static line is generated by Monte-Carlo binomial
sampling rather than the closed form, which automatically captures the small bias
of the discrete estimator at low $n$ and at the $E\to0,1$ edges; the two agree in
the interior.

## Reading the 2-D (E, s_E) plot

The BVA plot shows every burst as a point $(\bar{E}, s_E)$, usually as a 2-D
density (KDE or histogram) with the static line $\sigma_\text{sn}(E)$ overlaid:

- **On the line.** A population whose density sits *on* the static semicircle is
  **static** on the burst timescale: its only variance is shot noise. A folded
  protein, a rigid dye-labeled duplex, or a donor-only species all land here.
- **Above the line.** A population that sits **clearly above** the static line
  carries **excess variance** — the molecules interconvert between at least two
  FRET states *within* a single burst (millisecond and faster dynamics). The
  vertical displacement above the line grows with the amplitude of the FRET
  change and with the exchange rate relative to the burst duration.
- **Bridge between states.** Dynamic exchange between a low-$E$ and a high-$E$
  state produces a characteristic arch of elevated $s_E$ at intermediate $\bar{E}$,
  connecting the two static sub-populations — a signature of two-state kinetics
  even when the time-averaged FRET histogram shows only a single smeared peak.

BVA is deliberately **model-free**: it flags the *presence* of sub-burst dynamics
without assuming a kinetic scheme. It pairs naturally with methods that quantify
those dynamics — FRET-line analysis, dynamic-PDA, or {doc}`FRET-2CDE
</guides/01_fret_2cde>`, which tests the same hypothesis from a photon
kernel-density viewpoint.

## Choosing the slice size n

The slice size $n$ trades statistical stability against time resolution. Small
$n$ (few photons) gives many slices per burst and sensitivity to fast exchange,
but a noisier $s_E$ per burst and a taller static line. Large $n$ smooths $s_E$
and lowers the static line, but fewer slices per burst and blindness to the
fastest dynamics. Typical values are $n \approx 5\text{–}10$ photons per slice;
the same $n$ must be used for the data points and for the static line so the two
are directly comparable. ChiSurf can alternatively slice by a fixed **time
window** instead of a fixed photon count, useful when the count rate varies
strongly across the burst.
